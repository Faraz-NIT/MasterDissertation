# 1. Retail replenishment and the business case for change

## 1.1 A daily decision with consequences beyond the shelf

Retail replenishment begins with a practical question: how much should a store order today? Behind that question sit several competing expectations. Customers expect the product to be available. The retailer wants to avoid tying up money in stock that moves slowly. Suppliers need orders that respect delivery arrangements and carton sizes. For fresh products, the store also needs to sell the goods before they expire. An order can satisfy one expectation while making another harder to achieve. Buying more may protect availability, for example, but it can increase the amount that remains unsold.

The decision is repeated across many products and locations. A store manager might understand why one product needs extra stock, yet struggle to give the same attention to hundreds of order lines. Routine work includes checking what is already on the shelf, identifying deliveries that are still on the way, reviewing expected sales and applying supplier conditions. The workload grows when different suppliers express those conditions differently. The numerical order is therefore the end of a longer process of gathering information, interpreting it and deciding which rules apply.

Inventory research has long treated this problem as a balance between service and cost. Zipkin (2000) explains how demand uncertainty, delivery delays and the treatment of shortages shape the choice of inventory policy. These ideas remain relevant when a retailer introduces artificial intelligence. A language model does not remove the need to understand the delivery process or the cost of carrying stock. It changes one part of the information and decision process, which must still operate within the retailer's economic and physical constraints.

For a business school dissertation, the useful starting point is the decision itself. The question is whether a new arrangement helps the retailer serve demand and manage inventory responsibly. A technically impressive model can still be difficult to use, slow to respond or poorly aligned with an operating policy. Conversely, a modest model may be useful if it performs a clearly defined task reliably. This dissertation examines that second possibility: a small local language model working alongside conventional forecasting and numerical planning tools.

## 1.2 Why forecasting alone does not settle the order

A forecast estimates future sales. Replenishment turns that estimate into a purchase decision. The distinction matters because a good sales prediction can still lead to an unsuitable order. The store may already hold enough inventory, a delivery may be due tomorrow, or the proposed quantity may break a supplier's minimum-order rule. A decision that ignores these conditions can look sensible when considered only against the forecast. It becomes less sensible when the receiving team, supplier or finance department tries to execute it.

Consider an illustrative grocery store preparing for a busy weekend. Its forecast suggests that sales of a product will increase. The supplier sells that product in cartons of six and requires at least two cartons for an order. An additional delivery delay means that some of the forecast demand will occur before a new order can arrive. The manager needs to recognise these facts together. An order for seven units is unavailable under the carton rule. An order that arrives after the weekend also cannot satisfy the demand that occurs before its arrival. Neither problem is solved by improving the forecast alone.

Shared supplier capacity creates another complication. Several products can all need stock on the same day, while the supplier has a limit on what it can dispatch. The retailer then has to decide how to distribute the available capacity. Giving every product the same proportion of its requested quantity is understandable and easy to explain. It may, however, allocate stock differently from a planner that considers which shortages are most urgent. The allocation rule can influence customer service as much as the quality of the underlying sales estimate.

Van Donselaar et al. (2010) place automated replenishment in the context of actual ordering behaviour. Their work helps explain why the introduction of a system is also a change in a business process. Retailers need to decide how the recommendation fits with local knowledge, working routines and managerial responsibility. The present dissertation takes that process perspective. It evaluates a workflow that combines a shared forecast, an ordering tool and language-model roles, while retaining clear checks before an order is released.

## 1.3 The attraction of language models in this setting

Retail information does not always arrive in the format expected by a planning system. A supplier may send a short email announcing a new carton size. Another may provide a table of delivery conditions. A third may put the dispatch limit at the beginning of a memo and the minimum order at the end. People can usually recognise that these documents describe related business rules. A conventional planning tool, however, needs those rules in consistent fields before it can calculate a quantity. Converting a message into usable information is a plausible role for a language model.

The attraction is wider than document reading. A language model can also connect a business instruction to an approved tool. If a retailer announces a promotion and decides that availability should take priority, a coordination role can request the planning mode that reflects that instruction. A review role can examine the results of independent checks and return an approval or a hold. These are bounded tasks within a workflow. They let the model contribute through interpretation and coordination while the numerical tool remains responsible for arithmetic and the business constraints remain explicit.

This arrangement is relevant to managers because it resembles the division of work in a retail operation. A person who receives supplier information does not necessarily calculate every order quantity. A planner uses that information with stock and demand estimates. A reviewer then checks that the proposal can be released. The experimental system assigns these responsibilities to separate roles. All three language-model roles use the same underlying pretrained model, rather than three separately trained models. The distinction between a role and a model is important when interpreting both capability and computing requirements.

The appeal should also be examined carefully. A language model may produce a well-formed answer that contains a wrong number or selects the wrong business priority. A fluent response can make the error easy to miss. Successful document reading therefore does not automatically establish successful replenishment. The experiment measures each of these responsibilities separately and then assesses the consequences of the complete workflow. This allows the dissertation to discuss where the model helped without assuming that every successful inventory outcome was caused by the language-model component.

## 1.4 The management problem behind the research

The practical management problem is how to introduce language-model assistance without losing the strengths of existing replenishment methods. Retailers already have approaches for estimating demand and turning that estimate into stock targets. Replacing those approaches simply because a conversational model is available would require a convincing reason. A more measured approach is to identify a task where language understanding may be useful, connect that task to an established planner, and assess whether the resulting process maintains or improves operational performance.

This raises an attribution question. Suppose an agent-assisted system fulfils more demand than a simple ordering rule. The improvement could come from better allocation, more careful treatment of arriving stock or a different service priority. The language model may have contributed by reading the supplier message correctly, yet the numerical planner may explain most of the improvement. Managers need this distinction when considering an investment. Otherwise, a useful planning improvement can be presented as evidence for an additional model component whose value has not actually been measured.

The study therefore includes two comparisons. Conventional ordering provides an accessible reference for the performance of the complete package. A structured-data planner supplies the same numerical tool and current business rules without a language model. Its information is already organised in the fields that the tool needs. This second comparison asks whether adding the language-model roles changes the outcome once the planning capability is held constant. It is demanding, but it gives the research a clearer basis for explaining what the model adds.

The structured-data comparison also has a business limitation. In a real retailer, someone or some software would need to convert incoming messages into those fields. The experiment does not measure that effort, and it does not assume that structured information appears without cost in practice. The comparison isolates inventory performance, while the potential value of reducing manual interpretation remains a question for an operational pilot. This separation keeps an attractive business possibility visible without presenting unmeasured staff savings as an experimental result.

## 1.5 Research purpose and questions

The purpose of this dissertation is to evaluate whether a small local language-model workflow can participate reliably in retail replenishment, and to identify the contribution of that workflow relative to simpler and stronger references. The focus is a complete order process with supplier reading, planning coordination and review. The system is evaluated on selected retail sales series from M5 and FreshRetailNet-50K. FreshRetailNet is shortened to RetailNet in the figures so that the dataset labels remain easy to read.

The first research question concerns the complete workflow: how does an Ollama-based agent system perform against conventional ordering in customer-demand fulfilment and an inventory operating-cost measure? The second asks about the language-model component: what changes when the same planning tool operates with language-model roles instead of already structured inputs and a fixed priority rule? The third concerns reliability and context: how accurately do the roles interpret supplier rules and select business priorities, and how do the findings differ between the two retail settings?

These questions connect business performance to the mechanism that produces it. The first can identify whether the overall design is worth considering. The second can prevent an incorrect attribution of its benefits. The third helps identify which responsibilities need further attention. Together they are more informative than a single comparison that reports the total cost of an agent system and treats every reduction as proof of superior artificial intelligence. They also make it possible to report a useful outcome when the strongest evidence concerns interpretation rather than better numerical ordering.

The research is evaluative rather than a claim of commercial deployment. It develops a defined system, applies it to historical data in a disclosed simulator and assesses the resulting evidence. There are no retailer interviews, staff observations or live purchase orders in the study. The findings concern the selected panels and operating assumptions. This boundary does not remove their relevance. It establishes what a manager can reasonably learn from the experiment and what would still need to be measured before making a production investment.

## 1.6 Two datasets, one common decision framework

M5 and RetailNet offer different settings in which to examine the same decision process. M5 provides a long history of daily retail sales and an established forecasting research context. RetailNet provides a fresh-retail setting with normalized sales and published information about stockouts. Both allow the construction of store–product sales series, yet they differ in their history, quantity conventions and the practical issues they highlight. Using both gives the dissertation a broader view than a single demonstration, while keeping the experiment small enough for local CPU inference.

The performance panel contains thirty selected series from each dataset. This is a computational study of sixty series in total, rather than an evaluation of every series in either source. The selection uses information available before the evaluation periods. Each dataset is assessed over two separate historical weeks, three operating situations and thirty simulation seeds. The same forecasting family and the same matched operating conditions are used across the three policies within a case. The design is intended to make policy differences interpretable rather than to compare unrelated settings.

In the simulator, M5 stock does not expire within the weekly horizon. RetailNet stock is assigned a hypothetical three-day usable life and consumed in first-in, first-out order. The shelf-life assumption adds an explicit availability–waste trade-off; it is not an observed product-level attribute supplied by the dataset. The raw quantity conventions also differ. M5 uses published unit sales, while RetailNet quantities are represented as published normalized sales multiplied by one hundred. Relative effects within a dataset are consequently more meaningful than a comparison of their absolute purchasing volumes.

The common framework helps identify both shared and context-specific findings. Supplier reading can be evaluated in the same controlled formats in both datasets. Inventory performance can then be interpreted in relation to the selected sales profiles and the declared physical assumptions. A stronger result in M5 does not mean that M5 is a better dataset, or that the language model is generally more intelligent in that setting. It means that the measured workflow produced a different effect under those particular demand patterns, reference policies and operating conditions.

## 1.7 Contribution and organisation of the dissertation

The intended contribution is a clear account of what a local language-model replenishment workflow can do, how that performance was measured and how its benefits should be attributed. The study connects operational outcomes with role-level behaviour rather than treating the agent system as an unexplained package. It also retains a strong reference that shares the numerical planning tool. This allows the positive evidence for supplier interpretation to be discussed alongside the limits of the measured inventory advantage from adding the language-model roles.

The dissertation follows the broad progression of the user-supplied master's thesis by Gilbert (2025): problem context, the role of data, the relevance of a model family, research development, findings, implications and future research. The reference is used for organisation and presentation. Its climate topic, empirical claims, figures and personal acknowledgments are not transferred into this study. The chapters are newly written around the replenishment question, and the completed Ollama experiment provides the quantitative evidence throughout the findings.

Chapter 2 explains the data and operational information needed for a credible replenishment decision. Chapter 3 introduces language-model roles and the relationship between interpretation, tools and authority. Chapter 4 sets out the experimental framework, including the datasets, forecasts, model and computing environment. Chapter 5 presents the main findings with separate M5 and RetailNet graphs and short explanations. Chapter 6 develops the business implications, including adoption choices and the conditions that an operational pilot should investigate. Chapter 7 gives a concise conclusion and a focused future-research agenda.

Detailed implementation settings, additional figures and evidence-location information are placed in the appendices. The main text aims to give a business reader enough information to understand the decisions, assess the comparisons and interpret the findings. It does not require the reader to follow software code or reproduce every calculation. At the same time, the supporting records make the study inspectable. Readability and research transparency serve different purposes, and the dissertation gives each a suitable place.
