# 2. Data and decision-making in retail

## 2.1 What the retailer needs to know

A replenishment decision draws on several kinds of information. Sales history helps estimate what customers may buy. The stock balance describes what can be sold now. Expected deliveries show what may become available later. Supplier conditions determine what can be ordered and when it may arrive. Spending authority determines which feasible purchases can be released. These inputs answer different questions, and they can come from different parts of the organisation. Combining them into a usable decision is therefore an information-management task as well as a forecasting task.

The stock figure illustrates why the meaning of a field matters. Stock on the premises may include goods that are already reserved, damaged or close to expiry. An incoming purchase order may describe goods that have been requested rather than goods that the supplier has confirmed. A manager may know the difference from working with the store, while a system receives only a number. The system needs a clear definition of what that number represents. Otherwise, arithmetic performed correctly on the wrong interpretation can produce a poor recommendation.

Wang and Strong (1996) argue that data quality extends beyond accuracy to the qualities that make data useful to their consumers. That perspective fits replenishment particularly well. A value can be accurate when recorded yet unsuitable for today's order if it refers to an old supplier agreement. A quantity can be complete yet difficult to use if its unit is unclear. Consistency across fields, availability at the decision time and relevance to the intended action all contribute to whether information is useful.

These considerations suggest a practical order of work. The retailer first needs to identify the source and meaning of each input, then decide how it will be used in the order process. A language-model role can assist with interpreting an incoming message, but it still needs a current source and an agreed field definition. The planning tool then needs those fields alongside the stock and forecast. The experiment follows this separation so that supplier-reading errors can be distinguished from the numerical consequences of an otherwise correctly specified plan.

## 2.2 Sales history and the limits of observed demand

Recorded sales show what a retailer sold, rather than every purchase customers might have wanted to make. When a shelf is empty, some customers may leave without buying the product. Others may switch to another product, defer their purchase or shop elsewhere. The sales record alone does not identify how many of these events occurred. This difference between sales and demand is especially relevant when the forecast is used to decide how much stock to carry in the next period.

An illustrative example makes the problem clear. Suppose a product sells ten units before running out at midday. The recorded daily total is ten. It does not follow that ten units would have met the full day's demand. However, it also does not follow that the missing demand was another ten units. The time of the stockout gives useful information about availability, but the quantity that customers would have purchased remains uncertain. A researcher who inserts a large estimated addition into the data would be making a modelling assumption rather than observing those purchases.

Nahmias (1994) discusses demand estimation in settings where unavailable stock causes lost sales. This literature explains why retail forecasts need careful interpretation when availability constrains the observations. The problem is not resolved simply by choosing a more complex prediction model. The target itself may contain a limitation. A model can learn the recorded sales pattern accurately and still provide an incomplete estimate of demand if frequent stockouts have shaped that pattern.

FreshRetailNet-50K was released specifically to support research into this issue. Its dataset card describes normalized daily and hourly sales, stockout-status information and contextual variables for fresh retail (Dingdong-Inc., 2025). Wang et al. (2025) describe the source as a stockout-annotated censored-demand dataset. That description makes the release useful for studying the relationship between observed transactions and availability. It also cautions against treating recorded transactions as an unrestricted measure of customer interest.

The present study uses released sales as its demand proxy and does not estimate unrecorded purchases. This choice keeps the experiment focused on replenishment coordination and avoids adding another inferred mechanism to the system. The resulting fulfilment measure refers to the demand world defined in the simulator. It should be read as the share of that declared demand that the policy satisfied. It is not an estimate of either retailer's actual historical service level, and the dissertation does not describe the study as recovering true latent demand.

## 2.3 Inventory information, timing and visibility

Inventory records carry their own uncertainties in a live business. Goods can be misplaced, damaged, sold through an unrecorded process or received under an incorrect code. DeHoratius and Raman (2008) provide an empirical basis for treating inventory-record inaccuracy as an operational concern. Kang and Gershwin (2005) also connect information inaccuracies with stock loss and stockouts. These studies support a general point: the quality of the stock information can influence performance even when the ordering rule itself is sensible.

The current experiment deliberately provides a consistent simulated physical ledger. It does not introduce corrupt inventory fields or attempt inventory-record repairs. The inventory literature is used to explain why clear information definitions matter, rather than to claim that this study tested those faults. Keeping that boundary visible prevents the dissertation from presenting an untested recovery capability as part of the new system. It also makes the research question easier to follow: the study examines what happens when a language-model workflow uses a known stock state and current supplier information.

Timing remains important even when the ledger is consistent. On-hand stock and stock on order are different. A delivery due after a promotion cannot meet sales that occur during the promotion. An order with a longer lead time may require a different stock target from an order that can arrive tomorrow. The planning process needs to use both quantity and timing. Considering all incoming stock as immediately available would make the decision appear better protected than it actually is.

The experiment records future deliveries separately from goods that have arrived. It also models delivery cancellations, partial fulfilment and occasional extra-day delays. These are disclosed simulation assumptions rather than observed supplier events. The same underlying delivery opportunities are used for matched policies. This allows the study to examine how different order decisions respond to equivalent supply conditions, while preserving a clear account of which parts of the operating environment came from the datasets and which parts were constructed for evaluation.

## 2.4 Forecasting as support for a decision

A sales forecast is useful because future demand is uncertain at the time the order is placed. Historical patterns can provide information about recent activity, weekly variation and changes in volume. The forecast does not promise that tomorrow will exactly repeat the past. It gives the ordering process a consistent estimate against which to plan. The choice of forecasting model should therefore reflect both its predictive purpose and the decision process that will consume its output.

The M5 competition demonstrates the importance of evaluating forecasting methods on a substantial retail benchmark. Makridakis et al. (2022) discuss the competition's results and the questions they raise for practical forecasting. For this dissertation, M5 contributes an established retail-data setting rather than a claim that the experiment reproduces the competition leaderboard. The study uses a small selected panel and a common forecasting method for all ordering policies. Its principal comparison concerns replenishment workflows, not a contest among many forecasting families.

Retail sales can be uneven. A product may sell on only some days, or a small number of large sales may dominate its recent history. Croston (1972) and Syntetos and Boylan (2005) show why intermittent-demand forecasting needs particular care. These contributions explain a difficulty that managers recognise immediately: a quiet sequence of days can coexist with occasional demand that is operationally important. A single average can hide that pattern. At the same time, a highly variable forecast may encourage an ordering process to hold more stock than it can reasonably sell.

The study uses LightGBM, a numerical machine-learning forecasting family, to provide the common sales estimate. The model uses historical sales patterns and calendar information available at the decision origin. It is fitted afresh for each dataset and each of the two weekly evaluation origins. The language model does not produce these forecasts. Keeping the forecasting family the same across policies means that a difference in replenishment performance cannot be explained by one policy receiving a different forecasting model.

Forecasting accuracy still matters as background context. A replenishment workflow cannot assume that an uncertain forecast has become a known future quantity simply because it comes from machine learning. Gneiting and Raftery (2007) highlight the importance of evaluating predictions in relation to their intended probabilistic meaning. The current study does not claim to estimate a fully calibrated demand distribution. It uses a shared point forecast and a training-derived uncertainty allowance within the disclosed planning rules. The forecasting context is retained so that inventory outcomes can be interpreted alongside the uncertainty of the common sales estimate.

## 2.5 Delivery delays, information changes and allocation

Lead time affects how long a store must rely on stock it already holds. If a delivery takes longer, an order placed today cannot immediately remove today's shortage. The retailer may need to hold a larger buffer, order earlier or accept that part of the demand cannot be met by that delivery. Clark and Scarf (1960) establish the importance of inventory-policy structure in systems with multiple stages. Although the present experiment is simpler than a full distribution network, the underlying lesson remains useful: inventory has to be planned in relation to when and where it becomes available.

Supplier conditions can also change within an evaluation period. An increased carton size changes the quantities a retailer is permitted to buy. A higher minimum order can make a small top-up unavailable. A reduced dispatch limit can require more careful allocation across products. These changes need to be communicated to the planner in a usable form. They provide a natural setting for the supplier-reading role, because the practical challenge is to recognise the current rule and preserve its meaning while expressing it as a field.

Lee et al. (1997) and Chen et al. (2000) discuss how information, forecasting and lead times can contribute to order variation through a supply chain. Their research provides a reason to look beyond the accuracy of a single forecast. Ordering behaviour can introduce additional variation when information is interpreted or acted on differently at successive points. The current dissertation does not claim to measure a multi-stage bullwhip effect. It uses this literature to motivate consistent information and clearly specified policy responses within one replenishment workflow.

Allocation creates a related management choice. A fixed supplier limit can make several individually reasonable orders jointly impossible. Proportionate allocation offers an understandable rule, but it does not always favour the products for which stock is most urgently needed. A constrained planner can consider different shortage priorities while keeping the same hard spending and supplier limits. The contrast between these approaches is part of the experiment. It helps explain why an agent-and-tool package may improve on conventional ordering even when the language model does not improve on the numerical planner alone.

## 2.6 Perishability changes the meaning of available stock

Fresh retail adds time to the definition of usable inventory. Two stores can hold the same quantity of a product yet face different ordering needs if one holds recently delivered stock and the other holds stock that will expire tonight. The older stock may satisfy today's demand while offering little protection for a delivery that arrives two days later. A replenishment system that considers only the total quantity can overlook this difference and underestimate the need for a future delivery.

Nahmias (1982) reviews the inventory problems created by perishability. The central business issue is that unsold stock can lose its ability to generate a sale. Carrying more inventory therefore has a consequence beyond the ordinary financing and storage burden. The retailer may eventually dispose of it. This gives managers a reason to assess availability and expiry together. A policy that raises service can still be unattractive if the additional availability comes with an unacceptable amount of waste.

The physical simulator uses first-in, first-out consumption for RetailNet. Older usable batches are consumed before newer ones, and remaining stock reaches the end of its hypothetical three-day life according to its batch age. This is a defined way of making age visible to the ordering tool. It does not establish the actual shelf life of the products in the released dataset, and it does not measure observed retailer disposal. The expiry results concern the modelled stock process used to evaluate the policies.

For M5, the simulator treats stock as nonperishable within the seven-day episode. This makes the comparison different from RetailNet even when the same broad ordering workflow is used. The difference is intentional and disclosed. It gives the dissertation a way to examine the role of an availability–expiry trade-off, while keeping the datasets' quantity scales separate. A lower expiry share in one policy is consequently a finding about the declared fresh-stock simulation, rather than evidence of an environmental saving achieved by either source retailer.

## 2.7 Separating observations, predictions and assumptions

Research on a business system becomes harder to interpret when every value is described simply as data. The sales histories in this study are released observations. The future sales estimates are model outputs based on earlier observations. The stock, supplier capacities and purchase events are constructed within a simulator. The current supplier messages describe that constructed environment. These elements can all inform a decision, but they support different kinds of conclusions.

This separation matters when the results are communicated. A forecast line in a graph should not appear to be an observed future quantity. A supplier dispatch limit should not appear to have been reported by the dataset publisher when it was created for the experiment. An operating-cost reduction should not be presented as a change in a retailer's audited accounts. Labelling each element helps a business reader distinguish what the study measured from the assumptions that made the measurement possible.

The supplier messages are controlled examples generated from current simulated contract records. They appear as ordinary prose, a reordered memo or a small table. The model is asked to identify carton size, minimum order, delivery time, dispatch capacity and the daily budget. There are no real supplier emails in the study. The variety provides a meaningful extraction task within a bounded setting, while leaving the interpretation of unrestricted commercial documents for future work.

The synthetic contracts are also available as authenticated current fields for independent verification. Checking a model's extraction against those fields does not give the model future demand information. It tests whether the interpretation agrees with the source that the message describes. The planner only proceeds when that agreement is established. This arrangement is particularly useful for examining the effect of supplier reading without allowing an incorrect field to become a silently accepted business rule.

## 2.8 What the performance measures mean to a manager

Customer-demand fulfilment is the most direct service measure in the study. It is the share of simulated demand that the policy can satisfy from usable stock. A higher percentage means that more of the declared demand was served. This definition makes the measure easy to connect to availability, while retaining its simulation boundary. It does not claim to measure customer satisfaction, brand loyalty or store visits, none of which are observed in the experiment.

The inventory operating-cost index combines holding stock, leaving demand unfulfilled and allowing stock to expire. It provides a common assessment rule within each dataset. The coefficients are explicit experimental choices, rather than estimates of the retailers' actual margins or disposal charges. Purchase spend is reported separately. This keeps the primary measure focused on the inventory consequences and avoids adding both acquisition and expiry in a way that would count the same goods twice without a complete commercial accounting model.

The cost index and service measure should be read together. A lower cost can be achieved by buying less, which may also reduce service. A higher service rate may require more stock, which can increase holding or expiry. Looking at only one outcome would conceal part of the trade-off. RetailNet expiry is therefore reported separately, and the study records held decisions and committed-action constraint violations as additional descriptions of how the process operated.

The two reference policies give these measures their interpretation. Against conventional ordering, an improvement describes the complete agent-and-tool package. Against the structured-data planner, a difference concerns the additional interpretation and coordination layer within that package. The research does not infer a universal benefit from either comparison. It asks what happened in the selected retail panels under a common declared evaluation world, then uses that evidence to identify the most credible role for local language-model assistance.
