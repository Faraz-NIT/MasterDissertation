# 6. Implications and recommendations

## 6.1 The strongest positive finding is a useful division of work

The study demonstrates that a small local language model can participate in a complete replenishment workflow. It read the controlled supplier messages correctly, requested the approved numerical tool and operated within a process that kept the actual order limits explicit. This is a meaningful result for a manager considering how language capabilities might fit into existing retail systems. It shows a practical way to assign the model a role without asking it to replace every established forecasting and ordering function.

The large M5 package improvement also shows why the workflow deserves attention. Availability was higher and the declared operating-cost measure was lower than under the conventional reference. The improvement was maintained across the three simulated business situations. For a retailer whose existing process resembles that reference, better projection and allocation could be valuable. The relevant investment question is then which changes explain the gain. The structured-data result indicates that the numerical planning capability should receive substantial attention in that assessment.

RetailNet provides a more restrained positive case. The agent package improved its average service and operating-cost measures against conventional ordering, while expiry changed differently across scenarios. This makes the result closer to the sort of trade-off a fresh retailer would need to examine. An attractive availability gain cannot be assessed independently of the stock that remains unsold. The common physical ledger and separate expiry measure make that trade-off visible, rather than allowing a single cost percentage to stand in for every business outcome.

Taken together, the datasets support a practical division of work. The language model can interpret supplier information; the numerical tool can turn verified information into constrained quantities; the gate can retain authority over release. The contribution is a functioning arrangement among these responsibilities. The experiment does not show that adding more conversational roles automatically improves inventory. A retailer can use the division as a design option while choosing the smallest set of model responsibilities that adds measured value to its own process.

## 6.2 Why attribution changes the investment decision

Attribution matters because different improvements require different investments. Improving a numerical planner may involve better stock projection, capacity allocation or integration with incoming deliveries. Adding a language interface may involve document handling, role instructions and review of extracted fields. If the retailer attributes the whole package improvement to the language model, it may prioritise the visible conversational component while leaving the more consequential numerical capability underdeveloped. The strong comparison helps avoid that mistake.

In M5, the agents and structured-data planner had essentially the same operational result. In RetailNet, the agents were slightly worse than that planner, particularly under promotion. The study therefore gives no inventory-performance reason to replace a capable structured-data planner with the language-model layer when the information is already available in suitable fields. A retailer with well-integrated supplier records can reasonably retain its existing structured process while testing language assistance only where the information arrives outside that process.

The potential benefit of document interpretation remains commercially relevant. Someone has to read a supplier memo, identify the current terms and make those terms usable by the planner. A model that carries out that conversion reliably could change the effort required. However, the current study supplies the structured reference directly and does not measure the human work behind it. An investment case would need to include the time taken to prepare fields, the cost of reviewing exceptions and the consequences of errors in the actual communication environment.

This gives the dissertation a more specific business conclusion than a general claim that artificial intelligence improves replenishment. The planning capability produced the strongest measured inventory gain. The model performed a useful interpretation task in the tested corpus. Its additional financial value remains to be measured against the real cost of producing and maintaining structured information. These statements can all be true together, and distinguishing them helps a manager compare the components on the responsibilities they actually perform.

## 6.3 Fixed business rules and the limits of model discretion

The priority-selection result identifies a responsibility that needs particular care. Reading numbers correctly did not guarantee that the coordination role followed the expected promotion policy. This matters because a priority can change quantities even when the resulting order is feasible. Supplier and budget checks can prevent overspending or an invalid carton size, yet they do not establish that the plan reflects the retailer's intended service preference. Policy correctness and numerical feasibility therefore need separate treatment.

Where a business rule is already explicit, it may be better implemented directly. If the retailer has decided that every active promotion uses a particular planning mode, the system can apply that mapping without asking a model to reconsider it on every decision. The structured-data planner in this experiment did exactly that. This does not mean language assistance has no role in recognising an announcement or clarifying its meaning. It means the final mapping from a verified event to a firm policy can remain a controlled business rule.

RetailNet makes this point concrete. The model's overall priority score was lowered by its promotion decisions, even though routine and supplier-disruption priorities were selected correctly. A generally high overall score could have concealed this weakness if the results had not separated the conditions. Managers should consequently ask for evaluation on the decisions that matter most to their process, including promotion activation and exceptional delivery terms, rather than relying on an average across many straightforward routine cases.

The experiment does not identify a single cause for the dataset difference in priority selection. Context values, wording and the small model configuration may all be relevant, but they were not independently varied to estimate their effects. A later study could investigate those factors. The immediate recommendation is supported by the existing evidence: retain firm policy mappings where they are available, and assess model-assisted interpretation on the actual events that precede those mappings. This focuses the model on a language task while keeping a declared operational preference consistent.

## 6.4 Designing a pilot around the work that is missing

The next useful pilot would examine the part of the business case that the simulation could not measure. The retailer would identify a set of actual incoming supplier communications and the process currently used to convert them into planning inputs. A language-assisted version could then be compared with that existing process. The comparison would assess field accuracy, completion time, review effort and the handling of exceptions. It would also retain the same numerical planner so that information-processing value is not mixed with a simultaneous planning upgrade.

A shadow phase is appropriate as a first step. The system would prepare interpretations and recommendations while the retailer's existing process continued to determine live orders. Staff could compare the outputs without granting the new workflow independent purchasing authority. This would provide examples of ambiguous terms, missing information and differences in commercial conventions that do not appear in the controlled corpus. The proposed phase is a recommendation, not an activity performed in this dissertation.

If the shadow results were satisfactory, a reviewed pilot could permit a defined subset of orders with explicit human release. The retailer could choose stable suppliers and products with clear ordering conditions, then monitor whether the model changes review workload or decision quality. A subsequent phase could consider routine bounded release for cases that meet established criteria. Each phase would have its own purpose and completion evidence. Movement between phases would depend on measured performance, rather than on the fact that a previous stage produced convincing demonstrations.

The pilot should measure the complete information process. A model that extracts quickly but creates many ambiguous cases may transfer work to the reviewer rather than remove it. A model that handles routine messages accurately may still need expensive support when supplier formats change. Conversely, it may reduce repetitive preparation even when the final purchase quantities remain identical to those from the current planner. That possibility explains why unchanged inventory results do not by themselves settle the business case for a language interface.

## 6.5 Measuring value beyond a simulated cost index

The experiment's operating-cost index gives a declared preference for availability, holding and expiry. It is useful for comparing policies under common assumptions. A retailer's financial appraisal would need a fuller account. Acquisition prices, margins, fixed ordering costs, storage, disposal, markdowns and closing inventory value can all influence whether an order is economically attractive. The coefficients used in the simulator cannot be treated as the retailer's measured values for these items.

The model layer introduces additional operating costs. They include computing capacity, software integration, monitoring, exception review and the work required to maintain the connection between supplier information and the planning system. The study records local decision time, but it does not price those activities. A financial case should compare the total process cost of the current and proposed arrangements, including the effort saved and the effort added. A percentage reduction in the inventory index alone does not establish return on investment.

Service can also have business consequences that the simulator does not measure. A fulfilled sale may support customer retention or reduce substitution, while an unavailable item may have a different consequence depending on the retailer and product. The stockout literature explains why these effects are plausible, but this study does not observe them. An operational pilot could examine relevant customer and store outcomes with an agreed measurement design. The present recommendation is to keep those outcomes distinct from the demand-fulfilment ratio that the experiment actually calculates.

For fresh retail, the same care applies to environmental claims. The RetailNet expiry measure makes wasted modelled stock visible, and its scenario differences are relevant to planning. It does not measure kilograms of food waste, disposal practices or emissions. Those quantities would require actual product and operational information. The study can motivate including waste in an adoption decision without presenting a simulated expiry reduction as an achieved sustainability result. This is a more credible starting point for a business case that includes both service and resource use.

## 6.6 Work design, visibility and managerial responsibility

Introducing an agent workflow changes how responsibility is expressed in the process. The supplier role produces fields. The planner proposes quantities. The reviewer evaluates checks. The gate permits release. A manager needs to understand these responsibilities well enough to identify where a failure occurred and which part of the system should be adjusted. Naming the roles is useful only if the names correspond to actual recorded behaviour and defined authority.

The experiment provides that visibility through saved messages, model outputs, proposals and physical outcomes. In a retailer, a similar record could help explain why an order was placed or held. The useful explanation would concern the current rule, selected priority, forecast and release checks. It need not depend on a lengthy model-generated account of its internal reasoning. Turpin et al. (2023) provide a reason to be cautious about treating a language model's explanation as a reliable account of the cause of its answer. Recorded inputs and checks offer a more inspectable basis for operational review.

Human review should also have a defined purpose. Asking a person to approve every output without a clear reason can create a repetitive task in which genuine exceptions receive little attention. Parasuraman and Riley (1997) describe problems of automation use and misuse that are relevant to this issue. A retailer can design the review process around the items that actually require judgement: unclear supplier terms, unusual priority requests or cases that fall outside the approved operating range. The numerical checks can handle the rules that are already precise.

This approach does not claim that the dissertation measured staff trust or reviewer effectiveness. It uses the literature and the observed role differences to identify a practical work-design question. A future pilot would need to test whether the evidence presented to staff makes review easier and whether the division of responsibilities remains understandable in real operating conditions. The experiment supports a transparent process architecture; the organisational effect of using it remains a separate empirical question.

## 6.7 Computing choices and practical scale

The CPU-only result is relevant because it shows that the complete selected-panel study could be executed with a small local model. The configuration did not require a GPU or a paid external language-model service. Local execution also made it possible to retain the full requests and responses within the study environment. This supports the feasibility of assessing bounded language roles under a modest computing arrangement, while leaving production infrastructure and support requirements outside the demonstrated result.

The observed mean latency is tied to the experimental design. A decision covers thirty series through one portfolio request to the numerical tool, rather than making a separate language call for every product. The supplier reader handles three group contracts, and the coordinator selects among a limited set of priorities. These scope choices explain why the call count could remain manageable. The same timing should not be multiplied mechanically to claim a capacity for all fifty thousand RetailNet series or a retailer with much longer and more varied documents.

A production design might also call the model at different points. Current supplier terms may remain valid for many days, while a new announcement may require immediate interpretation. Choosing when to use the model is therefore part of the operating design. The clean experiment made fresh calls every agent day so that the role behaviour was fully evaluated and no answer cache concealed mistakes. It did not investigate the business effect of reducing calls, changing batch size or reusing verified contract fields. Those are reasonable questions for a later, separately evaluated system.

The computing decision should follow the task and the evidence. A larger model may follow difficult policies more reliably, but it can add inference time or hardware cost. A simpler role may avoid that cost while preserving the interpretation benefit. The current results favour testing such choices against the actual responsibility assigned to the model. They do not support selecting a larger system solely because it offers more general conversational capability than the bounded replenishment process requires.

## 6.8 What the two-dataset study contributes

The two datasets strengthen the analysis by showing both a large package gain and a modest one. M5 makes the planning improvement visible, particularly under difficult supplier conditions. RetailNet shows a reference policy that is closer to the stronger planner and adds a short-lived-stock trade-off. The language role that read supplier fields performed consistently in both, while priority selection did not. This combination gives the discussion more substance than a single successful example would provide.

The research also makes a distinction that is useful beyond these panels: successful language interpretation and better numerical replenishment are different claims. The model can contribute the former without establishing an advantage in the latter. Keeping the same planning tool in the strong reference gives that distinction empirical support. The dissertation can consequently describe the tested LLM roles positively while retaining a clear account of where the operational improvement came from.

The main remaining limits concern transfer. The messages were controlled, the supplier events and costs were synthetic, and each dataset contributed only two weekly episodes. The selected panels reflect the declared eligibility and volume-stratification rules. The model configuration was fixed and the source periods had been used in earlier project work. These boundaries limit a broad claim of superiority, yet they also identify the next evidence that would be valuable: real communication variation, a longer operating horizon and measured incremental information-processing cost.

The recommendation is to begin with a capable planner and reliable business definitions, then evaluate language assistance where interpreting incoming information creates a real workload. Keep supplier and spending limits outside the model's discretion. Apply firm business-policy mappings directly when they are already known. Use a staged pilot to measure the additional value, including cases where purchase quantities remain unchanged. This recommendation follows the strongest observed capabilities and the clearest observed weakness of the completed study.
