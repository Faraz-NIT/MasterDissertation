Abstract
Retail replenishment depends on more than an accurate demand forecast. Inventory records,
incoming orders and supplier terms must also be reliable enough to support an executable
decision. This dissertation examines how large language model (LLM) agents can assist that
process while remaining subject to explicit operational controls. It develops a hybrid architecture
in which language models interpret bounded document evidence and request tools, while
deterministic components certify the state, generate forecasts, optimize quantities and decide
whether execution is permitted.

The design is informed by retail standards and the literature on inventory, data quality and
automation. Two completed computational studies used thirty selected M5 item-store series with
simulated inventory, suppliers and disturbances. The first comprised 360 numerical runs across
thirty simulation seeds, eight two-day prose feasibility runs and four structured-rule controls. The
second evaluated four matched parser/LLM and recovery/no-recovery configurations over
fourteen days, two seeds and four scenarios, producing 32 runs and 448 decision traces.

The numerical study showed the importance of evidence controls: under a missing-feed
disturbance, the ungated optimizer recorded 63 hard-constraint violations, compared with none for
its gated counterpart. The initial local LLM configuration failed to produce an executable
replenishment decision because its constraint extraction was incomplete or dimensionally
incorrect. After the extraction task was narrowed and independently checked, the second study
recorded 186 nonzero replenishment actions across the model-enabled arms and no committed-
plan hard violations. Verified recovery of corrupted derived inventory reduced mean simulated
cost from USD 894.30 to USD 796.51 and raised fill rate from 89% to 96%.

These findings support a practical role for LLM agents within a governed replenishment
workflow. They do not establish an additional economic advantage from the language model: the
strongest parser achieved the same operational results, and only ten of twenty model recovery
choices followed the instruction. The contribution is a demonstrated way to combine bounded
language interpretation with verifiable decisions, together with a managerial framework for
deciding where such assistance is worth its cost. Short horizons, synthetic supplier documents and
two seeds in the second study limit generalization; field return on investment and human-audit
benefits remain untested.

Executive Summary
For a retail manager, the attraction of an LLM agent is its ability to work with the information that
sits around an optimization model: a supplier note, a changed limit, an exception in an inventory
feed or an explanation requested by a planner. The experiments examined whether that assistance
could be incorporated without weakening the controls over purchase and transfer decisions. The
results show that it can, provided the language model has a narrow task and its output remains
subject to independent checks.

The clearest operating improvement came from restoring usable evidence. In the fourteen-day
pilot, a simulated integration error caused a derived stock field to collapse even though the
underlying source and movement records remained available. Verified recovery reduced average
portfolio cost by USD 97.79, or 10.93%, and improved fill rate by seven percentage points. The
recovery tool also refused to repair stale-feed cases where current evidence was absent. This
distinction matters commercially: recovering a demonstrably wrong calculation is different from
inventing missing stock information.

The LLM-assisted system maintained the same cost and service outcomes as a capable
deterministic parser. Its bounded extraction calls returned all 76 submitted numeric terms
correctly, and the two model-enabled configurations executed 186 nonzero replenishment actions
without a recorded hard violation. However, the model selected recovery incorrectly in ten stale-
feed cases. Independent eligibility checks blocked those requests. Managers should therefore view
the successful execution boundary as a property of the whole control system, rather than as
evidence that the model itself is consistently reliable.

The adoption case is strongest where business information is genuinely difficult to express
through maintained rules: changing supplier language, exceptions that span several documents,
and the preparation of evidence for review. Those broader advantages were not measured here. On
the controlled documents used in this study, a parser was equally effective and required no
language-model calls. A sensible business decision would retain that parser for routine clauses,
use the LLM selectively for a demonstrated information gap, and compare its additional benefit
with inference, review and monitoring costs.

This dissertation consequently recommends a staged approach. Establish source lineage and state
checks first; formalize the main constraints second; introduce trace-based review third; and pilot
LLM assistance with approval before considering bounded execution. Success should be judged
on service, total operating cost, time to resolve exceptions and harmful execution, alongside
model accuracy. A convincing explanation is useful only when the underlying decision can be
checked.

Chapter 1: Introduction

1.1 Replenishment as a Sequential Decision under Uncertainty

Inventory replenishment is a repeated business decision made under uncertainty. A retailer has to
estimate future demand, understand what stock is actually available, account for goods already on
order, respect supplier and budget constraints, and decide how much to buy before the consequences
of that decision are fully known. Ordering too much ties up working capital and can lead to
markdowns, obsolescence, or waste. Ordering too little creates stockouts, lost sales, and poor customer
experience. The decision is therefore not simply about forecast accuracy; it is about balancing service,
cost, cash, and operational risk over time.

Classical inventory theory provides a strong foundation for this problem. Base-stock policies, (s, S)
policies, multi-echelon models, and stochastic dynamic programming provide transparent rules under
clearly defined assumptions (Clark & Scarf, 1960; Zipkin, 2000). These models remain valuable
because they make the trade-offs explicit and, unlike a generative model, they can guarantee feasibility
when the assumptions and inputs are valid. Retail environments, however, regularly challenge those
assumptions through changing assortments, intermittent demand, promotions, uncertain lead times,
pack-size restrictions, supplier minimums, and incomplete information about stock and open orders.

1.2 Two Gaps: Semantic and Evidential

Modern retailers already have access to strong forecasting and optimization methods. Probabilistic
forecasting models can estimate distributions rather than point forecasts, while mathematical
optimization can convert those forecasts into order quantities subject to capacity, budget, pack, and
service constraints. Yet two practical gaps remain between a model output and an executable purchase
order.

The first is a semantic gap. Business constraints are not always stored as clean parameters. A
minimum order may be defined at supplier level rather than SKU level; a case-pack rule may use a
different unit from the selling unit; an assortment decision may determine whether an item is eligible
for a store at all; and a temporary capacity change may arrive in a document rather than in a database
field. The challenge is not only to read these rules, but to translate them into precise, testable
constraints.

The second is an evidential gap. The optimization model normally treats its inventory position, open
orders, prices, and sales history as facts. Research on inventory record inaccuracy shows that this
assumption is often unsafe. DeHoratius and Raman (2008), for example, found substantial
discrepancies between recorded and actual inventory in a large retail sample, while other work shows
that even modest inaccuracies can materially affect automated replenishment decisions (Kang &
Gershwin, 2005; DeHoratius, Mersereau, & Schrage, 2008). Data-integration research adds another
layer: when information is assembled from heterogeneous systems, quality problems can arise through
missing values, duplicates, failed ETL stages, inconsistent keys, stale data, or inconsistent
representation (Rahm & Do, 2000; Woodall et al., 2016; Souibgui et al., 2019).

This dissertation argues that agentic automation in replenishment has to address both gaps. An AI
system should not merely understand the rule and optimize the order. It should first establish whether
the evidence on which the order is based is sufficiently trustworthy.

1.3 Operational Grounding from Retail Standards and Prior Research

The operational design of this study is grounded in a synthesis of retail standards, supply-chain
research, and data-quality literature. The ARTS Operational Data Model provides a retail-specific
view of items, supplier relationships, sales transactions, inventory, receipts, and transfers, and is
explicitly intended to support integration across retail applications (Object Management Group, n.d.).
GS1 standards make a complementary distinction between relatively stable master data, business
transaction data, and time-stamped visibility events, while the GS1 Global Data Model standardizes
foundational product attributes used across the product life cycle (GS1, 2022, 2026). Together, these
sources provide a defensible basis for the common entities and lineage concepts used in the
dissertation's canonical schema.

The literature also suggests that the reliability of those entities cannot be taken for granted. Wang and
Strong (1996) frame data quality as broader than accuracy alone, including dimensions such as
completeness, timeliness, and consistency. Research on ETL and data integration shows that
heterogeneous sources introduce duplicate records, missing values, schema conflicts, transformation
errors, and delays (Rahm & Do, 2000; Yang, Ge, & Helfert, 2017; Souibgui et al., 2019). In retail
operations, inaccurate inventory records are particularly important because an automated ordering
policy can repeatedly act on a false stock position (DeHoratius & Raman, 2008).

These findings lead to three design choices that recur throughout the dissertation. First, the
replenishment system operates on a common, versioned decision state rather than reading directly
from heterogeneous source systems. Second, data quality is treated as an operational input to the
decision rather than as a separate reporting concern. Third, a decision trace records not only the
recommendation but also the data version, constraints, model outputs, and verification steps that
produced it. In other words, the research starts from a managerial question—when should the
organization trust automation enough to let it act?—and turns that question into a technical control
architecture.

1.4 The Promise and Perils of LLM Agents in Supply Chains

LLM agents are attractive in this setting because they can work across the boundary between
structured computation and unstructured business information. ReAct demonstrated how a language
model can alternate between reasoning and external tool use (Yao et al., 2023), while AutoGen
formalized multi-agent collaboration with different roles and tools (Wu et al., 2023). In supply-chain
settings, systems such as OptiGuide use a language model as an interface to established optimization
tools rather than as a substitute for them (Li et al., 2023). More recent work explores multi-agent
inventory decisions, consensus, memory, and solver-assisted repair (Quan & Liu, 2024; Jannelli et al.,
2024; Yoshizato et al., 2026; Ao, Simchi-Levi, & Wang, 2026).

At the same time, the risks are significant. Agentic models can be inconsistent across repeated runs,
can reproduce human-like biases in inventory decisions, and can generate convincing explanations that
do not faithfully reflect the cause of the output (Turpin et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2025; Long et al.,
2026). Documents supplied by external parties can also contain instructions that function as indirect
prompt injections (Greshake et al., 2023). These limitations suggest a conservative design principle:
the LLM should interpret and coordinate, but deterministic tools should decide whether the numbers
are valid, the constraints are satisfied, and the action is permitted.

1.5 Problem Statement and Scope

The research problem is therefore not whether an LLM can generate an order quantity. It is whether an
agentic system can combine the flexibility of language models with the discipline of formal decision
tools while remaining governable in an operational setting. The system must be able to work with
imperfect evidence, translate heterogeneous business rules into formal constraints, distinguish between
low-risk and high-risk decisions, and leave an audit trail that a manager can verify after the fact.

The dissertation focuses on operational replenishment for item-location-supplier combinations in a
multi-period, multi-store setting. It considers stochastic and intermittent demand, uncertain lead times,
pack multiples, supplier minimums, assortment eligibility, capacity and budget constraints, lateral
transfers, and data-quality degradation. It does not attempt to solve strategic network design or the full
pre-season fashion-buying problem. Autonomous execution is evaluated only in simulation; the
managerial recommendation for real organizations is staged adoption with approval gates.

The completed empirical work is narrower than this overall problem statement. It covers three
FOODS items across ten stores, a numerical benchmark and a subsequent controlled agent pilot.
The full multi-origin, severity and human-review programme remains a research agenda. Chapter
5 states which comparisons were actually completed, and Chapter 6 separates observed findings
from propositions that still require testing.

1.6 Research Questions and Hypotheses

The central research question is: Can an LLM multi-agent replenishment system deliver useful
decisions while granting autonomy only when the underlying evidence and constraints have been
sufficiently verified?

This question is developed through seven sub-questions.

  •     RQ1 — Performance. How does the proposed system compare with classical and
        forecast-plus-optimizer baselines on total cost, service, stockouts, and bullwhip
        amplification when the data state is clean?

  •     RQ2 — Evidence quality. When the observed state is degraded, does evidence-gated
        autonomy reduce harmful executed orders relative to systems that continue operating with
        fixed autonomy?




                                               Page 7 of 90
                                                             Evidence-Gated Autonomy for Retail Replenishment



  •     RQ3 — Constraint grounding. How accurately can the system translate realistic
        replenishment constraints—such as pack sizes, supplier minimums, unit conversions, and
        assortment eligibility—into typed, verifiable rules?

  •     RQ4 — Architecture. What value is added by specialist roles, typed shared state, critic
        review, and memory, and what coordination cost do these mechanisms introduce?

  •     RQ5 — Reliability. How often does the system violate hard constraints, fail to use tools
        correctly, or produce materially different outcomes across equivalent runs?

  •     RQ6 — Trace faithfulness. Can the decision trace be replayed and tested so that the
        stated evidence genuinely corresponds to the action taken?

  •     RQ7 — Human audit. Does a structured trace with lineage help reviewers detect poor
        decisions more quickly and with better calibrated trust than a free-form explanation?

The hypotheses are deliberately framed so that they can be falsified.

  •     H1. A tool-grounded multi-agent system will outperform an LLM-only policy on
        operating cost and reliability, while remaining close to a well-specified stochastic
        optimizer when the data are clean.

  •     H2. Independent critic checks will reduce hard-constraint violations and high-cost tail
        events, but will increase latency and computational cost.

  •     H3. Typed shared state will reduce grounding errors and invalid tool use relative to free-
        form agent communication.

  •     H4. Under state-quality disturbances, evidence-gated autonomy will reduce the harmful-
        execution rate relative to fixed-autonomy variants. Deterministic checks will capture most
        simple failures; language-model reasoning will add most value where multiple sources or
        documents must be reconciled.

  •     H5. Constraint extraction will be strongest for explicit, templated rules and weaker for
        coupled constraints, unit conversions, and ambiguous document conventions.
        Deterministic validation and escalation will prevent most residual errors from reaching the
        optimizer.

  •     H6. Structured traces will achieve higher replay consistency, evidence completeness, and
        counterfactual sensitivity than free-form rationales.

  •     H7. Reviewers given lineage-bearing traces will identify decisions made on degraded
        evidence more accurately and more quickly than reviewers given only narrative
        explanations.

  •     H8. Under severe demand, supply, or data shocks, bounded autonomy with escalation will
        produce higher safety-adjusted utility than unrestricted autonomous execution.

The research questions and hypotheses are retained from the revised design so that the reader can

1.7 Contributions and Structure

The dissertation makes six connected contributions. It develops a compact, literature-informed
replenishment schema; makes evidence quality part of the permission to act; implements a hybrid
interpretation-and-tool workflow; creates transparent M5-based simulation conditions; evaluates
recorded decisions through integrity checks and selected replays; and translates the findings into a
staged adoption framework. The empirical contribution includes both a failed first LLM
configuration and a revised configuration that supported actual replenishment. Retaining the
failure is important because it shows why the controls and the narrower task were necessary.

The business contribution is therefore a conditional adoption argument. LLM agents can be useful
participants in replenishment when their role matches an information problem and their output can
be verified. The numerical benefit observed here came from evidence recovery, which was
available equally to the parser and LLM configurations. Demonstrating a workable language
interface and demonstrating that this interface creates additional commercial value are separate
steps.

The original eight-chapter structure is retained. Chapters 2 and 3 establish the literature and
operating context; Chapter 4 explains the architecture and the implemented pilot; Chapter 5
reports the executed design; Chapter 6 presents results and managerial implications; Chapter 7
addresses governance and limitations; and Chapter 8 draws the conclusion. The appendices
contain the full run tables, every graph from the two final study reports, and the reproducibility
record.

Chapter 2: Literature Review

2.1 Inventory Control Theory and the Bullwhip Effect

Inventory theory provides the economic core of this dissertation. At its simplest, replenishment
balances the cost of holding stock against the cost of running short. In multi-period and multi-location
settings, that trade-off becomes more difficult because today's order changes tomorrow's inventory
position and can affect other parts of the supply chain. Clark and Scarf (1960) established important
results for multi-echelon systems, while later work consolidated practical policies such as base-stock
and (s, S) control (Zipkin, 2000).

A second strand of literature shows why locally reasonable decisions can create instability at system
level. Sterman (1989) demonstrated how decision delays and misperceptions generate oscillation in
the Beer Game. Lee, Padmanabhan, and Whang (1997) described the bullwhip effect through demand-
signal processing, order batching, rationing games, and price variation, and Chen et al. (2000)
quantified the role of forecasting and lead time. This matters for AI-enabled replenishment because a
policy should not be judged only by the accuracy of one order. A system that appears efficient item by
item may still generate excessive upstream volatility.

Recent research suggests that agentic AI does not automatically remove this problem. Long et al.
(2026) report that stochastic LLM decision-making can itself become a source of order variability,
producing what they call an agent bullwhip effect. The implication for this dissertation is
straightforward: reliability must be evaluated over repeated runs and over the full sequence of
decisions, not inferred from a few plausible examples.

Lateral transshipment provides another useful control mechanism. When stores belong to the same
network, a local shortage can sometimes be addressed by moving inventory rather than placing a new
supplier order. Paterson et al. (2011) review a substantial literature on this topic. The simulator
therefore allows transfers between eligible locations as a secondary action, which makes the setting
closer to a networked retail decision than a single-stockpoint problem.

2.2 Inventory Record Inaccuracy, Censored Demand, and Data Quality

The quality of the recorded state is central to automated replenishment. DeHoratius and Raman (2008)
found that a large share of audited store-item inventory records in their retail sample differed from the
physical quantity. Kang and Gershwin (2005) showed that such discrepancies can have an outsized
effect on stockouts because an automated system may continue to believe that stock is available when
it is not. DeHoratius, Mersereau, and Schrage (2008) further show that explicitly accounting for record
uncertainty can improve both replenishment and audit decisions. Together, these studies justify
treating inventory accuracy as part of the decision problem rather than as a separate control activity.

Sales data create another evidential problem. A sale records a transaction, not necessarily the full
demand that would have occurred if stock had always been available. In lost-sales settings, demand is
censored during stockouts, which can bias forecasts downward (Nahmias, 1994). Intermittent demand

adds further difficulty because many item-location series contain long sequences of zeros, for which
traditional forecasting methods can perform poorly (Croston, 1972; Syntetos & Boylan, 2005).

Information-systems research broadens the discussion beyond inventory accuracy. Wang and Strong
(1996) argue that users assess data through dimensions including accuracy, completeness, timeliness,
and consistency. Data-integration research reaches a similar conclusion from a process perspective:
heterogeneous sources create missing values, duplicates, inconsistent representations, schema
conflicts, and transformation failures, so data quality has to be managed throughout the integration
workflow rather than checked only at the end (Rahm & Do, 2000; Yang et al., 2017; Souibgui et al.,
2019). Woodall et al. (2016), studying ETL deployments in large organizations, report failures that
affect timeliness, accessibility, and representational consistency. These findings motivate the state
certificate introduced in Chapter 4.

2.3 Probabilistic, Hierarchical, and Intermittent Retail Forecasting

A replenishment decision needs more than a point estimate of next week's sales. The uncertainty
around the forecast determines safety stock, service probability, and the economic value of waiting or
escalating. Probabilistic forecasting is therefore a better fit for the decision problem. DeepAR learns
probabilistic forecasts across related series (Salinas et al., 2020), while the Temporal Fusion
Transformer combines multi-horizon forecasting with variable selection and attention mechanisms
(Lim et al., 2021). More recent time-series foundation models such as Chronos and Moirai provide
useful zero-shot or lightly adapted baselines (Ansari et al., 2024; Woo et al., 2024).

The M5 competition is particularly relevant because it combines a large hierarchy, intermittent item-
level sales, prices, and calendar effects. It includes 30,490 bottom-level item-store series that
aggregate into 42,840 series, and its uncertainty track evaluates multiple forecast quantiles
(Makridakis, Spiliotis, & Assimakopoulos, 2022a; Makridakis et al., 2022b). Forecast coherence
across levels also matters in retail: item-store forecasts should not imply totals that contradict store,
department, or category forecasts. Trace-minimization methods provide one way of reconciling such
hierarchies (Wickramasuriya, Athanasopoulos, & Hyndman, 2019).

In this dissertation, the language model does not calculate a forecast in prose. The forecasting
component returns a versioned artifact containing quantiles or scenarios, calibration diagnostics, and
data-quality flags. This separation is intentional: generative models may help select or explain a
forecasting method, but the numerical forecast remains a tool output that can be reproduced and
evaluated independently.

2.4 Tool-Augmented LLMs and Multi-Agent Coordination

LLM agents combine a language model with tools, memory, observations, and an action loop. ReAct
demonstrated a general pattern in which reasoning is interleaved with external actions (Yao et al.,
2023). AgentBench and AgentBoard show that performance in longer tasks depends on grounding,
planning, and recovery from intermediate failures rather than on answer quality alone (Liu, X. et al.,
2024; Ma et al., 2024). AutoGen provides a framework in which multiple agents can be given different
roles and tool permissions (Wu et al., 2023).


                                              Page 11 of 90
                                                               Evidence-Gated Autonomy for Retail Replenishment



These systems create opportunities but also new coordination risks. More agents do not necessarily
mean better decisions. Each additional hand-off can propagate an error, create inconsistent state,
increase latency, and make it harder to determine which component caused the final action. For a
business process such as replenishment, the question is therefore not whether a multi-agent design is
more sophisticated, but whether specialization produces measurable gains that justify its coordination
cost.

Planning research supports a hybrid approach. LLM+P translates natural language into a formal
planning representation and delegates search to a classical planner (Liu, B. et al., 2023). OptiGuide
similarly uses an LLM to interact with an established optimization model rather than replacing it (Li et
al., 2023). Simchi-Levi et al. (2025) argue for the use of LLMs as interfaces and coordinators around
quantitative supply-chain tools. This dissertation adopts the same principle: language models handle
ambiguity and orchestration; formal tools handle arithmetic, feasibility, and certification.

2.5 LLM Agents for Supply-Chain and Inventory Decisions

Early studies of LLM agents in supply chains show that the technology is capable of useful
coordination, but the evidence is still emerging. InvAgent applies a multi-agent approach to inventory
management (Quan & Liu, 2024), while Jannelli et al. (2024) study consensus-seeking among supply-
chain agents. Yoshizato et al. (2026) report benefits from structured decision prompts and retrieval of
previous cases. These studies support the use of specialization and memory, but they generally work in
settings where the state and the business constraints are already clean enough to be presented to the
agent.

Other studies highlight the need for stronger safeguards. AIM-Bench identifies decision biases in
agentic inventory managers (Zhao et al., 2025). OptiRepair uses solver feedback and explicit
inventory-theory checks to diagnose infeasible optimization models (Ao et al., 2026). The lesson is
important for this dissertation: an LLM judge should not be the final authority on whether a
replenishment plan is operationally valid. Validation should be expressed through tests that can be
executed independently of the model.

2.6 Explainability, Decision Traces, and Faithfulness

Business users often ask an automated system to explain why a recommendation changed. A natural-
language rationale can be helpful, but it can also create false confidence. Research on chain-of-thought
faithfulness shows that a model can produce a fluent explanation that does not faithfully reveal the
factors that caused the answer (Turpin et al., 2023; Arcuschin et al., 2025). This makes explanation
quality a governance issue rather than only a usability issue.

The dissertation therefore distinguishes between a rationale and a decision trace. A rationale is text. A
trace is a structured record of the evidence and tools used to make the decision: the snapshot, forecast
artifact, active constraints, solver result, critic tests, approval status, and execution receipt. The text
shown to a reviewer is generated from that record rather than treated as evidence in its own right.
Faithfulness can then be tested by replaying the decision, removing evidence, or changing a cited
factor and checking whether the action responds as expected.

2.7 Human–Automation Interaction and Graded Autonomy

Human-factors research has long shown that automation can be misused, disused, or over-trusted
(Parasuraman & Riley, 1997). Parasuraman, Sheridan, and Wickens (2000) describe different levels of
automation across information acquisition, analysis, decision selection, and action implementation.
This framework is useful for AI governance because it avoids treating automation as a binary choice
between manual and fully autonomous work.

The architecture in this dissertation follows that logic. A decision can remain advisory, require explicit
approval, execute automatically within a bounded policy, or—in simulation only—operate fully
autonomously. The level is recomputed for each decision. This is a meaningful departure from
assigning a fixed autonomy level to an entire product category: the same item may be safe to reorder
automatically on one day and require human review on another because the evidence has deteriorated.

2.8 Research Gap

The literature has developed strong methods for forecasting, formal inventory optimization, data-
quality management, and increasingly, LLM-based coordination. Retail standards also provide mature
representations for product, supplier, transaction, and inventory information. What remains
underexplored is the connection between these areas. Existing agentic replenishment studies rarely
make the quality of the observed state an explicit input to the autonomy decision, and explainability
studies rarely test whether an operational decision can be reconstructed from its evidence lineage.

The gap addressed here is therefore an end-to-end governance problem: how to combine semantic
flexibility, quantitative rigor, evidence quality, and accountable automation in one replenishment
process.

3.1 Setting and Method

This chapter translates the literature reviewed in Chapter 2 into a concrete representation of the
information that a replenishment system needs. It is intentionally based on published research and
established retail data standards rather than on the internal architecture of any one company. The
objective is not to claim that all retailers use the same database design. It is to identify a defensible
minimum set of business objects and quality risks that recur across retail operations and that can
therefore support a controlled research artifact.

The synthesis uses four types of evidence. First, the ARTS Operational Data Model provides a mature
retail-specific vocabulary for items, vendors, sales, inventory, receipts, and transfers, and is explicitly
designed to help retailers integrate operational applications (Object Management Group, n.d.). Second,
GS1 standards distinguish master data, transaction data, and supply-chain visibility events, while the
Global Data Model standardizes foundational product attributes and the EPCIS standard captures what
happened, when, where, and in what business context (GS1, 2022, 2026). Third, the academic
inventory literature identifies decision variables that matter for replenishment, including inventory
position, lead time, demand uncertainty, pack sizes, supplier constraints, assortment decisions, and
transshipment. Fourth, the data-quality and ETL literature identifies common risks introduced when
information is integrated across heterogeneous systems (Wang & Strong, 1996; Rahm & Do, 2000;
Woodall et al., 2016; Yang et al., 2017; Souibgui et al., 2019).

This approach is consistent with design-science research: the artifact is built from a clearly defined
problem environment, but the design decisions are made traceable to prior knowledge rather than
justified through personal experience alone (Hevner et al., 2004). The resulting schema and failure
taxonomy should therefore be read as a literature- and standards-informed research abstraction. Where
the literature provides strong empirical evidence—such as inventory record inaccuracy—that evidence
is cited directly. Where a failure mode is a logical implementation stress case rather than a well-
quantified population phenomenon, it is labelled and evaluated through sensitivity analysis rather than
presented as an observed frequency.

3.3 How Replenishment State Is Produced

A replenishment system rarely observes the physical world directly. Its decision state is assembled
from systems such as point of sale, e-commerce, enterprise resource planning, warehouse
management, supplier feeds, and planning applications. Data-integration research commonly
represents this process as extraction from heterogeneous sources, transformation into a common
structure, quality checking, and loading into a target analytical or operational model (Rahm & Do,
2000; Yang et al., 2017; Souibgui et al., 2019). The exact technology may be batch ETL, ELT,
streaming, APIs, or a combination, but the governance problem is the same: the decision system sees
the result of an integration process, not the underlying reality.

For the purpose of this dissertation, the state-production process is represented through five general
steps. Master-data alignment maps products, suppliers, and locations into common identifiers.
Transaction ingestion loads sales, purchase orders, receipts, and transfers. State construction derives
the current inventory position and the time series used for forecasting. Quality certification tests
freshness, completeness, consistency, cardinality, units, and referential integrity. Versioning freezes
the state used for a decision so that the decision can later be replayed.

This model is deliberately technology-neutral. A retailer with streaming inventory and a retailer with
nightly batches may produce different freshness distributions, but both still face the need to know
which sources were current, which transformations succeeded, and which version of the state informed
the order. The simulator therefore uses daily snapshots because the M5 data are daily, not because
daily batch processing is assumed to be universal.

3.6 Decision Context Specifics

The literature suggests several features that make retail replenishment different from a generic
forecasting exercise. Assortment changes mean that a zero sale does not always mean zero demand;
an item may simply not be ranged at that location. Pack sizes can force stores to hold more inventory
than a continuous optimization model would recommend (Das et al., 2021). Inventory inaccuracy
means the system's stock position may be uncertain. Lost sales mean demand becomes partly
unobserved during stockouts. Transfers create an alternative to new purchasing. Finally, managerial
overrides are not necessarily irrational: research on store ordering shows that local managers often
modify automated recommendations because the formal model does not capture all operational
constraints (van Donselaar et al., 2010).

These observations are important for the business-school framing of the study. The aim is to
understand how an organization can combine formal decision support with human judgment while
retaining control over the evidence behind a decision. The completed experiments assess
operating outcomes, exception handling and software auditability. Audit time, reviewer workload
and calibrated human trust remain proposed evaluation outcomes rather than reported findings.

Chapter 4: Methodology and Architecture

4.1 Research Design

The study adopted a design-science approach because the research problem concerned both a
managerial need and an artifact intended to address it (Hevner et al., 2004). The artifact combined
data-quality checks, probabilistic forecasting, formal optimization, bounded language
interpretation and an explicit autonomy policy. Its evaluation proceeded in two stages: an initial
numerical and live-model feasibility study, followed by a revised agent pilot responding to the
first stage's grounding failures.

The initial study established a useful distinction. Strong replenishment tools could operate on
structured rules, but the small local language model could not reliably complete the larger
extraction task from prose. The revised design therefore assigned fewer decisions to the language
model. It retained deterministic interpretation where the document grammar was known, asked the
model for a small number of quoted numeric terms, and used independently checked tools to bind
those terms to business metadata.

The second stage used a matched four-configuration design: parser without recovery, parser with
recovery, LLM without recovery, and LLM with recovery. This made it possible to separate the
effect of inventory recovery from the effect of adding a language model. All configurations
received the same observable source evidence and the same trained forecaster, optimizer and
deterministic critic. The model did not receive clean evaluator state or future sales.

Development and evaluation were separated within each stage, with model settings, prompts,
input versions and solver parameters recorded before the relevant evaluation. The revised pilot
was nevertheless an adaptive engineering study informed by the initial failure. It was not an
independent preregistered confirmation of the dissertation hypotheses. The planned human-audit
component was not conducted.

distinction for this dissertation is between the true state used by the simulator to evaluate an outcome
and the observed state made available to the decision system.

The CVaR term follows Rockafellar and Uryasev (2000) and gives additional weight to poor tail
outcomes rather than optimizing only the average case. This is particularly relevant when evaluating
agentic systems because a system with a good mean result but occasional extreme failures may be
unacceptable in practice.

The design introduces two additional constructs. The first is the autonomy policy, which maps the
evidence available for a decision into a permission level:

  a[t] = pi(q[t], r[t], kappa[t], sigma[t])
       in {advisory, approval, bounded, full}

  q   evidence / state-quality score
  r   business risk of the action

  sigma forecast uncertainty

The wider design also specifies safety-adjusted utility so that a policy is not rewarded merely for
avoiding every difficult decision. The reported implementation supplies a descriptive utility proxy
combining operating cost, a run-level tail-cost diagnostic and configured penalties for escalation
and harmful execution. It is useful for comparing the registered simulator policies, but its penalty
weights were not estimated from a retailer's actual review workload or losses.

  U = -(expected cost + lambda * CVaR)
    - cEsc * number of escalations
    - cHarm * number of harmful executions

The simulator distinguishes two kinds of undesirable outcome. A hard violation means that a
committed action breaks a true constraint used by the post-decision evaluator. A clean-reference
deviation means that the action differs materially from a numerical reference decision; such a
difference is not, by itself, proof of business harm. The stored harmful-execution proxy can
include these deviations. Chapter 6 therefore reports hard violations, holds and reference
deviations separately rather than treating them as interchangeable measures.

4.3 Blackboard Architecture over a Canonical Schema

The agents share a typed, versioned decision state rather than exchanging only free-form messages.
This pattern is sometimes described as a blackboard architecture: specialist components read from and
write to a shared representation of the problem. In this dissertation, that shared representation is the
canonical schema developed in Chapter 3.

The architectural choice has a governance purpose as well as a technical one. Every object contains
units, timestamps, provenance, validity windows, and a decision identifier. Downstream tools
consume those structured fields rather than relying on prose. A forecast produced from snapshot A
cannot silently be combined with inventory from snapshot B. If evidence from different versions must
be combined, the system has to record a reconciliation step.

The canonical interface is also what makes the experiment independent of any one retailer's
technology stack. In a real deployment, ERP, WMS, POS, e-commerce, and supplier systems would
be mapped into this layer. In the experiment, M5 and the simulator are mapped into the same layer.
The agents therefore operate on the business concepts defined by the research design rather than on
vendor-specific source tables.

4.5 Coordination Protocol

The workflow follows a simple sequence: OBSERVE → CERTIFY STATE → FORECAST and
GROUND CONSTRAINTS → PROPOSE → VERIFY → COMMIT → EXECUTE →
MONITOR. Forecasting and constraint grounding can run in parallel once the state has been certified.
The system does not proceed to an executable proposal when the state contains an unresolved hard
failure.

Each stage has an explicit entry and exit condition. Typed schemas are validated before a hand-off.
Units are normalized. Tool calls use idempotency keys where duplication would be harmful.
Timeouts, retry budgets, and maximum dialogue turns prevent open-ended agent conversations.
Agents have allowlisted tools and limited write permissions. These controls are ordinary software-
engineering mechanisms, but in an agentic architecture they also serve as governance controls because
they restrict what the model is capable of changing.

The architecture can support a separately labelled deterministic fallback when policy permits. The
completed LLM studies used a hold-on-failure boundary and did not substitute a hidden parser for
a rejected model term. This distinction is essential for interpretation: a held model decision
remains a held decision, and a deterministic control is not counted as an LLM success.

4.6 State Reconciliation and the Data-Quality Score

The state certificate is computed over the exact evidence needed for a decision. It therefore does not
ask whether the entire enterprise data warehouse is healthy; it asks whether the inventory, open orders,
sales history, price, and eligibility used for this order are sufficiently reliable. The certificate covers
the following families of checks:

  •     Freshness: whether each relevant source is recent enough for the decision horizon.

  •     Completeness: whether expected item-location, order, and reference records are present.

  •     Continuity: whether the historical series contains unexpected gaps.

  •     Cardinality and uniqueness: whether duplicate facts or orders are present.

  •     Referential integrity: whether products, suppliers, and locations resolve correctly.

  •     Units and ranges: whether quantities, prices, and conversion factors are plausible and
        compatible.


                                               Page 24 of 90
                                                             Evidence-Gated Autonomy for Retail Replenishment



  •     Cross-source consistency: whether overlapping sources disagree beyond an allowed
        tolerance.

  •     Movement consistency: whether the change in stock can be reconciled with sales,
        receipts, and transfers.

  •     Distribution shifts: whether a transformation failure has created an implausible system-
        wide change.

  •     Censoring flags: whether sales observations coincide with stockout periods.

Each test returns pass, warning, or hard failure together with evidence. Warnings are combined into a
quality score; a hard failure can force a hold regardless of the numerical score. The LLM may then
help interpret warnings that require context—for example, whether a sudden disappearance of
transactions is more consistent with a closed store or a broken feed—but it cannot directly change the
underlying data or override a hard failure. Its interpretation is recorded as a hypothesis, not as a new
fact.

The revised pilot added a recovery route before a suspect state could proceed. Recovery was
allowed only for a derived-inventory collapse accompanied by a movement inconsistency, with a
complete and current source inventory observation that agreed with the recorded movement
ledger. The tool restored permitted derived quantities, retained the original observation and issued
a new state certificate. It did not infer missing sales or use the simulator's hidden true state.

A stale or incomplete feed failed those preconditions. The right response was to hold, because
agreement with old information cannot establish the current stock position. Both parser and LLM
configurations had access to the same recovery evidence. This prevented an information
advantage from being mistaken for a language-model advantage.

4.7 Constraint Extraction and Verification

The full design represents a constraint through its entity, scope, parameter, value, unit,
conversion, aggregation, validity, source and precedence. The first live-model study asked the
model to extract this larger tuple from controlled prose. It revealed dimensional errors and
missing terms, so no model-grounded proposal reached the optimizer.

The V2 pilot used a more limited division of labour. A capable parser handled the 155 supported
routine clauses. The model was used for supplier capacity and portfolio budget terms, returning
only a source reference, numeric value, unit and a short verbatim value quote. Where a capacity
term changed, the new source version required a fresh interpretation rather than reuse of an
obsolete cache entry.

Independent validation checked the numeric value, unit and quoted source span before tools
attached the entity, scope, validity and other structural fields. Those fields were tool-derived
metadata, not fields the language model had independently understood. Wrong or omitted numeric



                                             Page 25 of 90
                                                             Evidence-Gated Autonomy for Retail Replenishment



terms were not silently replaced. A bounded correction attempt was permitted, although none was
required in the completed V2 extraction calls.

Verified results were cached against the document version, model and context. A cache hit was
reuse of an earlier interpretation, not a new model trial. This reduced repeated inference while
retaining evidence of what had originally been submitted and returned. The method is appropriate
for the disclosed document grammars; it cannot be assumed to work unchanged on natural
supplier contracts.

4.8 Forecasting Module

The executed numerical benchmark used three distinct forecast/policy combinations: seasonal-
naive demand scenarios for B1, global LightGBM quantiles for B2, and a native recurrent
probabilistic model for B3 and B4. The recurrent model used a gated recurrent unit with a
negative-binomial demand likelihood. It was not a reproduction of published DeepAR or
Temporal Fusion Transformer results.

Separate descriptive backtests covered seasonal naive, Croston-SBA, LightGBM and the native
recurrent model. The preferred pre-study holdout origins were 1760 and 1788; later origins 1800
and 1828 were retained as overlapping descriptive evidence. Scores were calculated for the
selected thirty-series panel and its 112 aggregate nodes, not for the full M5 competition hierarchy.

The V2 comparisons shared one saved recurrent model. It used a 56-day lookback, a hidden
dimension of 32, ten training epochs and at most 12,000 training windows. It supplied demand
scenarios to the same optimizer in all four configurations. This kept forecasting differences from
being attributed to the LLM.

Chronos, Moirai and the Temporal Fusion Transformer remain relevant literature and proposed
extensions. They were not executed as replenishment baselines in these completed studies.
Hierarchical scenario aggregation was used for the backtest scores; no comparative MinT
reconciliation experiment was completed. The LLM did not generate the numerical forecasts.

4.9 Optimization and Policy Tools

The optimization layer selects the simplest appropriate method. For an uncoupled item with a clear
state and straightforward pack constraint, a policy calculator may be preferable to a full mathematical
program. When supplier minimums, shared capacity, budgets, or transfers link several items or
locations, a mixed-integer model is used.

The LLM does not calculate the objective value or certify feasibility. It supplies a validated problem
specification to the optimization tool and interprets the result for the workflow. If the model is
infeasible, solver diagnostics are used to identify conflicting constraints. The agent may suggest a
resolution, but a constraint that comes from an authenticated contract or policy cannot be relaxed
automatically. This follows the solver-in-the-loop principle used in work such as OptiRepair (Ao et
al., 2026).

The registered solver used a seven-day planning horizon, four sampled scenarios, a five-second
time limit and a relative mixed-integer gap of 0.001. The portfolio budget was USD 3,000; risk
weight was 0.1 with a 0.95 tail-risk level. Orders were bounded at 1,000 units per item-location
line, and eligible transfers were enabled. Supplier capacity was also subject to the active source-
derived constraints, including the capacity-cut scenario.

Purchasing, fixed order, transfer, holding, shortage and spoilage costs were included within the
decision window. Purchases were charged when ordered, and remaining stock received no
terminal salvage credit. The short-window total is therefore a simulated operating-cost measure,
not a retailer profit or return-on-investment calculation.

4.10 Evidence-Gated Autonomy and Safety Controls

The architecture uses four levels of autonomy. Advisory means the system proposes an action but
cannot submit it. Approval means a user must accept or modify the proposal. Bounded autonomy
allows automatic execution within predefined limits. Full autonomy is reserved for simulation or
tightly controlled testing.

The autonomy level is recomputed for each decision using the evidence-quality score, action risk,
constraint confidence, and forecast uncertainty. A routine low-value order with fresh data, stable
demand, and high-confidence constraints may qualify for bounded execution. The same item can be
downgraded to approval if the inventory snapshot is stale or a supplier rule has changed. A hard state
failure results in a hold and an escalation packet.

A hold can prevent a bad order and still carry a service cost. The implementation recorded a hold
budget and escalation penalties; the completed V2 study used approval mode hold, without a live
human approver. A decision requiring approval consequently remained held. The experiment did
not vary review staffing, approval delay or hold budgets to estimate an optimal escalation service
level.

The evaluated controls included typed inputs, source references, unit checks, pack and eligibility
checks, spend limits, deterministic critic review, state-version checks and idempotent simulator
receipts. These were local simulation controls. Authenticated supplier integration, operational
least-privilege enforcement and externally immutable audit storage remain deployment
requirements; local hashes and chains should not be described as providing those guarantees.

4.11 Native Structured Decision Trace with Lineage

Every recommendation creates a structured decision record. This is not a transcript of hidden
reasoning. It is an audit object composed of observable inputs, tool outputs, tests, and approvals. A
human-readable explanation is generated from that record after the decision has been assembled.

  1.    Identity and timing: decision identifier, item-location-supplier, timestamp, policy
        version, and granted autonomy level.



                                            Page 27 of 90
                                                             Evidence-Gated Autonomy for Retail Replenishment



  2.    Lineage: state version, source freshness, invariant results, quality score, and unresolved
        anomalies.

  3.    Evidence: source identifiers or hashes, reconciliation results, forecast version, quantiles,
        and uncertainty status.

  4.    Constraints: normalized values, units, scope, source, validity interval, confidence, and
        whether the constraint was active.

  5.    Alternatives: candidate order and transfer quantities, cost and service trade-offs, and
        rejected options.

  6.    Verification: critic tests, stress checks, vetoes, revisions, and residual uncertainty.

  7.    Commit and execution: approval or override, idempotency key, execution response, and
        later outcome events.

A trace is considered complete only if the final action can be reconstructed from the referenced state,
constraints, and tools with pinned versions. This requirement turns explainability into a testable
property rather than a stylistic feature.



For the revised pilot, the exact model request was stored before the HTTP call and paired with its
later response or error. The record therefore included unsuccessful attempts and semantic
validation, as well as accepted results. Imported cached calls were distinguished from fresh
physical calls. Authorization headers were excluded from the evidence export.

The independent audit checked content hashes, event-chain ordering, request/response pairing and
the recorded context for explicitly forbidden evaluator fields. These checks support the integrity
of the observed workflow. They do not prove that a model's internal explanation is faithful, nor do
they protect against an actor who could rewrite the complete local store.

Chapter 5: Experimental Design and Evaluation Protocol

5.1 Data: The M5 Retail Hierarchy

The empirical benchmark is the public M5 retail dataset. It contains daily unit sales for 3,049 items
sold across ten stores in three US states from 29 January 2011 to 19 June 2016, together with weekly
selling prices and a calendar of events and SNAP-related days (Makridakis et al., 2022a). The bottom-
level item-store series form a twelve-level retail hierarchy, making M5 useful for testing both item-
level decisions and aggregate consistency.

M5 is an appropriate benchmark for three reasons. It is large enough to support heterogeneous demand
patterns, it contains the price and calendar information needed for realistic forecasting, and it has a
well-documented evaluation tradition. It also has clear limitations for this dissertation: it does not
contain inventory, suppliers, purchase orders, lead times, or assortment plans. Those elements are
therefore simulated transparently and are not presented as observed facts.

Each completed stage used a single decision origin, with training data ending before its warm-up
and evaluation period. Multiple origins were used only in the separate forecasting backtests.
Policy comparisons used matched seeds and exogenous opportunity streams; the V2
implementation removed the policy label from the unknown-arrival opportunity key so that
equivalent business opportunities were sampled consistently across the compared configurations.

The completed experiments selected FOODS_1_001, FOODS_2_001 and FOODS_3_001 across
all ten stores, producing thirty bottom-level series. This was a deliberate small portfolio, not a
random or department-balanced sample of M5. Historical sales were used as a demand proxy; the
analysis does not claim to observe demand that was lost before the original sales were recorded.

The numerical decisions covered 2–8 February 2016. The two-day feasibility and structured-rule
comparisons covered 2–3 February 2016. Their registered training cutoff was 18 January 2016.
The V2 decisions covered 1–14 March 2016, with training ending on 15 February 2016 and a
fourteen-day warm-up. These are historical data dates. Cloud execution and document preparation
took place in October 2026.

5.3 Disturbance Layers

The simulation added supplier/constraint, assortment and state-quality layers because those
operational facts were absent from M5. Supplier rules and wholesale economics were generated
by the research environment. They were exposed as structured rules or controlled prose with
disclosed rendering grammars. These documents represent an extraction test, not a validated
sample of commercial contracts.

Three scenarios were executed in the initial numerical study: normal operation, derived-field
collapse and feed gap. The prose feasibility comparisons used normal operation and derived-field
collapse. The V2 pilot added a fourth scenario, supplier capacity cut, registered before evaluation
outcomes were observed.

Derived-field collapse set a calculated inventory quantity to an implausible value while leaving an
observed source quantity and movement evidence available in V2. Feed gap made relevant
evidence insufficiently current. Capacity cut changed a supplier limit while leaving the need for
formal feasibility checks intact. The V2 evidence-recovery tool could repair the first condition,
but could not legitimately repair the missing-feed condition.

5.4 Inventory Initialization and Simulation State

Because M5 provides sales rather than inventory, a warm-up period is used to create a plausible
starting state. Initial stock is based on lead-time demand and a service-level safety factor. Open orders
are seeded from the same demand and lead-time process. During each simulated period, the
environment updates receipts, sales or lost sales, transfers, true on-hand inventory, open orders, and
the versioned observed state presented to the policy.

Initialization was part of the simulation rather than a reconstruction of the original stores' stock.
The V2 warm-up lasted fourteen days. Sensitivity to alternative starting stocks and warm-up
lengths was not measured in the completed pilot. This is especially important for the original two-
day cases: initial inventory could satisfy demand even when the policy placed no replenishment
orders.

5.6 Evaluation Metrics

The evaluation deliberately combines technical and managerial outcomes.

  •       Forecast quality: WRMSSE, weighted pinball loss, CRPS, interval coverage, and
          calibration error.

  •       Inventory outcomes: total cost and its components, fill rate, cycle service level, stockout
          rate, inventory turns, days of supply, and transfer volume.

  •       Supply-chain dynamics: bullwhip ratio and upstream order amplification, including
          repeated runs with identical demand.




                                                     Page 32 of 90
                                                                  Evidence-Gated Autonomy for Retail Replenishment



  •       Evidence handling: detection precision and recall by failure class, time to detect,
          harmful-execution rate, escalation precision, hold cost, and calibration between granted
          autonomy and realized decision quality.

  •       Constraint grounding: exact-match accuracy for scope, value, unit, conversion,
          aggregation level, and validity interval; false-constraint rate; and the share of extraction
          errors that reach the solver.

  •       Agent reliability: valid tool-call rate, schema errors, hallucinated fields, constraint
          violations, latency, token usage, run-to-run variance, and tail regret.

  •       Human governance: review time, detection accuracy, override quality, stated confidence,
          and perceived workload.

Safety-adjusted utility is used as a summary outcome under disruption because it prevents a policy
from appearing superior merely by escalating every difficult decision.

The implemented outcome records included cost, fill rate, stockout frequency, cycle service, mean
inventory, window inventory turns, bullwhip, holds, executed actions, hard violations, clean-
reference deviations, model calls, tokens and latency. Forecast backtests recorded WRMSSE,
weighted scaled pinball loss, CRPS and 95% interval coverage. Appendix B reports the run-level
operational values and the complete backtest scores.

Fill rate is the proportion of demand units served; stockout frequency is a share of item-store-day
observations; cycle service is a run-specific cycle measure. They should not be substituted for one
another. Inventory turns refer to the short simulated window and are not annualized. Bullwhip and
daily tail-cost diagnostics are descriptive at these horizons.

Human review time, trust calibration, measured workforce savings, full failure-detection
precision/recall and organizational return on investment were not collected. Safety-adjusted utility
remains sensitive to the configured simulator penalties. The main conclusions consequently use
interpretable operating and safety measures rather than that proxy alone.

5.9 Human Audit Study

The human-audit study was not conducted. No participants were recruited and no measurements
of review time, trust, workload or detection accuracy were collected. This limitation applies to
RQ7 and H7, regardless of how complete the software audit records are.

The proposed extension retains a within-subject comparison of a free-form explanation, a
structured trace without lineage, and a full trace with lineage, subject to institutional approval.
Planners or suitably trained participants would review both sound decisions and known failures,
with formats presented in counterbalanced order. Review time, accept-or-override accuracy and
confidence would then establish whether the trace helps people rather than merely storing more
information.

For managers, the distinction is material. A technically reproducible decision record is useful
preparation for audit, but it does not demonstrate that a planner can use that record quickly or
correctly under operational pressure.

The analysis is descriptive and preserves the study boundaries. The numerical benchmark contains
thirty independent simulation seeds for each policy/scenario cell. Its first eight seeds were
extended by twenty-two disjoint seeds after the base results had completed; configuration and

trained-model equivalence were checked before the analytical union was used. This was an
exploratory increase in replication, not a confirmatory registration.

The V2 pilot contains two matched seeds for each configuration/scenario cell at one origin. Days

against the reference mean. No p-values, power calculations, non-inferiority tests or confirmatory

The earlier two-day prose cases and later fourteen-day hybrid cases differ in information,

correct attribution and the evidence still needed for a commercial claim.

6.1 Operational Performance under Clean State

Recovery did not improve the feed-gap results. Both configurations continued to average USD

important. A system that fills a missing stock field with a plausible number may appear
responsive but can create a larger purchasing error. In the tested workflow, the tool required
source-and-ledger agreement and refused to turn stale information into a current fact.

Under capacity cut, all configurations averaged USD 805.07 and 95.78% fill, with no committed
hard violation. The capacity source was reinterpreted when its version changed and the optimizer
respected the active rule. This provides a bounded example of maintaining an executable
constraint through a supply change, rather than evidence of handling every supplier disruption.

6.3 Constraint Grounding

The first live-model study is central to the dissertation's interpretation. Four B9/B10 runs

too demanding for the selected small local model and serving profile.

controlled replenishment pipeline.

Tool selection remained a separate weakness. The model followed the recovery instruction in ten
of twenty fresh choices and requested recovery incorrectly in all ten stale-feed cases. The
independent tool refused those requests and the gate held the decisions. The result suggests a
practical next design change: establish tool eligibility deterministically before offering a choice to
the model. That change is a recommendation, not an intervention evaluated in the frozen pilot.

6.4 Specialization, Typed State, and Coordination Cost

The revised pilot supports separation of responsibilities, but it does not prove that a larger
conversational team improves inventory performance. State reconciliation, numerical forecasting,
optimization, critic checks and execution were deterministic. The model was responsible for the
compact extraction step and the recovery request. No role-removal, free-form communication or
actionable-memory comparison was completed.

The business value of this separation is that each responsibility can be inspected and replaced
independently. A supplier term can be checked against its quote without asking the model to
certify its own interpretation. A purchase quantity can be recomputed without relying on the
model's arithmetic. A manager can also retain a parser for stable documents and introduce
language assistance only where that parser becomes costly to maintain.

6.5 Trace Faithfulness and Human Audit

The software audit provides a stronger basis for review than a narrative alone. It checked 130,430
original content-addressed objects and 130,542 chained events across the 32 V2 runs. All
discovered object hashes, chains, configured run identities and request/response associations
passed the recorded checks. The audit found no explicitly forbidden evaluator fields in the
captured model requests. It does not rule out every possible leakage channel or provide external
authentication.

Five selected decisions were replayed from copied evidence stores. All reproduced their recorded
state/certificate and action hashes. Three were executed decisions and two were held decisions.
Removing the saved forecast blocked reconstruction in the three executed cases; changing budget
or capacity to 1% changed the selected order without a new constraint violation.

These findings establish trace usefulness at the software boundary. They show that the observed
inputs and tools can reproduce selected recorded decisions. They do not reveal hidden model
reasoning, establish semantic dependence on every quoted document, or demonstrate superiority
over a free-form explanation in a randomized comparison.

6.6 Transferability and Managerial Implications

The results support an adoption sequence in which reliable evidence precedes greater autonomy.
The initial study showed that an agent can produce a plausible-looking extraction and still fail to
support an order. The later study showed that a narrowly tasked model can participate in actual
replenishment when independent tools establish the decision boundary. The useful managerial
question is where that participation solves an information problem that existing rules do not solve
economically.

Three aspects make LLM agents promising for replenishment. They can provide an interface to
supplier language that is inconvenient to encode manually; they can organize a bounded request
for specialist tools; and they can help present the recorded evidence to a planner. The pilot
directly demonstrated only the first aspect for a small controlled numeric task and the ability to
request a recovery tool. It did not measure manual encoding savings, broad exception resolution
or the quality of a planner-facing explanation.

The observed operating results nevertheless provide a sound basis for a cautious positive case.
The model-enabled workflow executed 186 nonzero replenishment actions and retained the
parser's cost and service outcomes. Its residual selection errors were contained by independently
applied conditions. This is a workable pattern for adding semantic assistance without delegating
unchecked purchasing authority.

Chapter 7: Ethical, Safety, and Governance Considerations

7.1 Automation Bias and Accountability

A polished AI recommendation can appear more authoritative than it deserves. Human-factors
research shows that users may over-rely on automated outputs, especially when the system presents
itself with confidence (Parasuraman & Riley, 1997). The design therefore treats explanation as
evidence for review rather than as a tool for persuasion. Failed checks, uncertainty, alternatives, and
escalation reasons should be shown alongside the recommended action.

Accountability also requires a clear record of who or what made the final decision. The trace
distinguishes between a system-generated proposal, a human-approved order, a human-modified
order, a held decision, and a bounded-autonomous execution. Procurement policy remains an
organizational responsibility even when the computational steps are automated.

The stale-feed selections provide a concrete example of why accountability must sit outside the
model. Ten tool requests were inconsistent with the instruction, yet none was allowed to become a
repair or an executed order. A commercial rollout should preserve that independent permission
boundary and name the owner of any policy override. A manager should be able to see that a
recommendation was refused without having to infer the refusal from a long conversation log.

7.2 Data Quality as a Safety Property

Evidence gating shifts risk from acting on bad information to delaying an action. In the V2
collapse scenario, holding without recovery reduced fill to 89%, while verified recovery restored
it to 96%. A hold was therefore a temporary control, not an adequate replenishment strategy by
itself. The implemented penalty and hold budget do not establish an optimal human escalation
process; a retailer would also need an owner, deadline and service target for resolving the
exception.

The dissertation uses only public benchmark data and synthetic operational layers. No confidential
retailer sales, supplier terms, or production telemetry are required for the reported experiment. This
improves reproducibility and reduces privacy and commercial-confidentiality concerns.

7.3 Security and Prompt Injection

Supplier documents, spreadsheets and operational notes should be treated as untrusted inputs.
Indirect prompt injection can influence an LLM-enabled application through retrieved content
(Greshake et al., 2023). The prototype used typed outputs, role boundaries, source references and
an input screen, but the current pilot did not complete an adversarial evaluation. Its source labels
and local hashes were not authenticated supplier signatures. Authenticated ingestion, restricted
execution permissions and adversarial tests remain requirements for deployment.




                                             Page 50 of 90
                                                            Evidence-Gated Autonomy for Retail Replenishment



The simulator used idempotent execution receipts, and the language model did not submit
purchase orders to an external system. An operational implementation would need independently
enforced least privilege and approval controls at the actual execution endpoint. A successful local
gate is preparation for that integration, not proof that an ERP or supplier API is protected.

7.4 Supplier Concentration

Optimization can unintentionally reinforce supplier concentration when one supplier appears
consistently cheaper or more reliable. The issue is secondary in a replenishment study where many
products may be effectively single-sourced, but it remains a governance concern. Where supplier
choice exists, concentration should be measured and, if diversification is a policy objective,
represented explicitly as a constraint rather than expected to emerge from the cost function.

7.5 Environmental and Economic Externalities

Higher service levels can require more stock, storage, transport, and expedited shipments. Conversely,
poor replenishment can create waste through obsolescence or markdowns. Where data are available,
environmental costs can be added to the objective or reported separately. The computational cost of
agentic AI is also recorded through model calls, token usage, and latency so that the system can be
compared with simpler alternatives.

The experiment recorded calls, tokens and timing but did not measure energy use or carbon
emissions. Nor did it estimate actual retailer markdowns, waste or workforce effects.
Environmental and economic claims therefore remain conditional on future data. A retailer should
include those costs when comparing an LLM-assisted process with a parser-based workflow.

7.6 Limitations

The first limitation is the experimental environment. Only three FOODS items across ten stores
were selected from M5, and sales were used as a demand proxy. Inventory, suppliers, wholesale
economics, contracts and faults were simulated. The study cannot reconstruct the original
retailer's true demand, operating incidents or profitability.

The second is the limited coverage. The numerical study used seven days at one origin, and the
model-feasibility cases only two. The V2 pilot extended the window to fourteen days but retained
two seeds and one origin. Initial stock, uncredited terminal inventory, shortage valuation and the
five-second solver limit can influence the apparent trade-off. Steady-state performance and a
complete severity matrix remain unmeasured.

The third is the language task. The documents were controlled grammars, and the V2 model
interpreted 76 numeric terms rather than all structural constraint fields. Exact performance on
those terms does not establish natural-contract understanding, general unit conversion or reliable
exception reasoning. Caching also creates correlated exposures rather than additional independent
trials.


                                            Page 51 of 90
                                                          Evidence-Gated Autonomy for Retail Replenishment



The fourth is attribution. The strongest parser matched every LLM operational comparison, and
the recovery improvement was available to both. Several changes separated V1 from V2, so the
development progression cannot identify the causal contribution of one prompt, cache or role.
Critic, memory and conversational-role ablations were not completed.

Finally, there was no field deployment, human participant study, measured organizational return
on investment or full adversarial evaluation. Selected cached replays verify recorded software
decisions, not latent reasoning. The research is a detailed and reproducible engineering
demonstration with descriptive operating evidence; its adoption framework still requires
organizational testing.

Chapter 8: Conclusion

8.1 Summary of Contributions

This dissertation investigated when an AI-assisted replenishment system has enough reliable
evidence to act. The completed work shows why that question matters. Numerical optimization
can be compromised by missing or corrupted information, and a language model can return a
valid-looking response that still cannot support an executable order. A trustworthy process has to
establish the state, validate the constraints and record the permission to act.

The experiments make a positive but bounded case for LLM agents in replenishment. After its
task was narrowed to source-quoted numeric terms, the local model participated in a workflow
that executed 186 nonzero replenishment actions across its two configurations, with no recorded
committed-plan hard violation. The system maintained the strongest parser's cost and service
outcomes. Those are useful feasibility results for incorporating language assistance into formal
inventory decisions.

The largest measured operating improvement came from verified evidence recovery. Under
derived-field collapse, mean cost fell by 10.93% and fill improved from 89% to 96%. Because the
same improvement occurred without an LLM, it should be understood as a benefit of the recovery
architecture. The model's ten incorrect stale-feed recovery requests also show why its authority
must remain conditional.

The dissertation contributes a literature-informed decision schema, a link between evidence
quality and autonomy, a tested hybrid interpretation-and-tool workflow, transparent M5
simulation conditions, reproducible trace evidence and a staged adoption framework. It does not
establish all the original hypotheses. It demonstrates that the proposed direction can work within a
controlled scope and identifies the comparisons needed to justify a wider commercial claim.

For a business reader, the conclusion is that LLM agents can be useful connective tools around
replenishment. Their strongest potential lies in reducing the friction between business language
and formal decision support. Organizations should preserve the numerical tools and independent
controls that make that assistance dependable, and add autonomy only after measuring the model's
incremental value.

8.2 Limitations

The findings are bounded by the selected public benchmark, the simulated operational layers and
the short evaluation windows. They cannot be transferred directly to fashion, luxury, industrial
distribution or an entire retail network. The V2 evidence comes from two seeds, controlled
supplier prose and one decision origin; it supports a feasible design, not a broadly validated
performance guarantee.



                                            Page 53 of 90
                                                           Evidence-Gated Autonomy for Retail Replenishment



The study also leaves important organizational questions unanswered. It did not measure whether
planners review the trace faster, whether supplier-term maintenance becomes cheaper, whether
the model resolves genuinely ambiguous exceptions, or whether the total process creates a
financial return after all costs. The exact parser match makes those questions central to the next
business case.

8.3 Future Directions

The next empirical step should compare the strengthened parser and the bounded LLM on a
validated set of naturally occurring supplier documents, including changing vocabulary,
conflicting versions, order-level minimums and unit conversions. The test should keep
information access and numerical tools equal, measure fresh interpretations separately from cache
reuse, and include the time required to maintain the parser. That comparison would address
whether language flexibility creates an additional benefit where this pilot could not show one.

A longer simulation should then extend the seeds, origins, disturbance severities and
replenishment horizon, with explicit treatment of terminal inventory and review delay. The model
should be offered recovery only after deterministic eligibility checks. Critic, memory and role
ablations would establish which parts of the agent architecture justify their complexity. These
changes should be registered before evaluation rather than treated as already proven
improvements.

The human-audit study remains essential. Experienced planners should compare the explanation
formats under realistic workloads, with review time, error detection, override quality and
confidence measured together. An approval-gated field pilot could follow, beginning with shadow
recommendations and recorded planner decisions before any automatic execution.

The architecture may also be useful in procurement, maintenance or workforce scheduling, where
quantitative decisions depend on fragmented evidence and textual rules. That transfer is a research
proposition. The present dissertation's concrete contribution is to show how semantic assistance
can be bounded, checked and connected to a reproducible replenishment decision.

This completed version retains the revised dissertation's title, eight chapters, principal section
order, literature review and original reference entries. Chapter 6 has been renamed from expected
findings to experimental findings. The abstract, methodology, evaluation protocol, governance
discussion and conclusion have been updated to reflect the studies that actually finished.

compact research abstraction informed by ARTS, GS1 and the cited inventory/data-quality
literature. No production-platform access, proprietary retailer telemetry or universal incident
calibration has been added.

feasibility cases, structured-rule controls and the revised parser/LLM recovery pilot. Benefits are
attributed to the mechanism that produced them. Human-audit, field-return and general language-
understanding claims remain future work.

repeated. Appendix D records configuration, development, audit and evidence-access details.

D.2 Development history and attribution

The first study selected a local Qwen2.5 1.5B model under the six-hour CPU constraint after
earlier development exposed weaknesses in smaller or alternative profiles. Its full-tuple extraction
failures remained in the final report. V2 development subsequently tested both Qwen 1.5B and
Llama 3B: both failed the larger tuple head in the first round, while the reduced numeric head
passed two of two development cases per model in the second round. Qwen was selected before
the revised evaluation. These small development cases are not additional independent evidence of
general model accuracy.

The first ninety-six numerical runs used seeds 0–7. The extension added seeds 8–29 after the base
results were available. Data, forecast-training tensors, LightGBM trees and numerical settings
were checked before forming the nonduplicated 360-run view. Earlier long studies, paused
checkpoints and superseded reports remain available as history; they are excluded from the 404
completed runs tabulated here.

V2 changes included compact grounded terms, trusted metadata binding, a capable parser
comparator, verified cache reuse, an independent recovery tool, a pre-HTTP request log and
consistent unknown-arrival opportunity sampling. Multiple changes and a new historical window
separate the stages. Their effects cannot be individually inferred from a before/after model
comparison.

D.3 Audit, software checks and reproducibility

The completed V2 audit checked 32 run identities, 448 decision traces, 68 fresh physical calls,
130,430 original content-addressed objects and 130,542 SQLite chain events. It also checked the
88-record workflow chain, 54 evaluated source/test pins, six prepared-data pins, two execution-
helper pins and the identity of the shared forecaster. All completed source-rule sets and both
recovery configurations were independently assessed. The software suite passed 218 tests, with
one optional Anthropic integration test skipped because its SDK was unavailable.

Fresh model requests were captured before transport and paired with their exact response or error.
Requests, cache imports and later evaluation artifacts were counted separately. The audit checked
the recorded context for forbidden evaluator keys and exact submitted-source equality. Zero client
errors does not mean zero semantic errors: ten recovery choices were instruction-inconsistent and
were blocked by the independent tool.

The selected cached replay checks were performed on copied stores. Three executed and two held
cases reproduced their recorded hashes; three forecast removals blocked reconstruction; and both
large numerical counterfactuals changed the selected action without a new violation. These tests
did not recall the model, inspect its internal reasoning or assess human understanding.

failures, all original SQLite histories, raw evidence objects, compact exports, earlier studies and
